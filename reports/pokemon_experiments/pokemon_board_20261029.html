Headline result · 27 Oct 2026 · the giant run's final checkpoint (4B steps)

A 9.6M-parameter recurrent policy, trained only by self-play (no human data, no search), beats every Metamon model we test on every test, including Metamon's best, Kakuna (143M): 54.8–57.5% vs Kakuna at piloting identical teams (+34 to +52 Elo, every interval above 50%), and 64.1% in the full game. As a pure free-pick drafter, building its own team from all of Gen 1, it beats Kakuna 74.3%, TaurosV0 79.5% and SyntheticRLV2 79.0%. On real Pokémon Showdown it beat Kakuna 68–32 and TaurosV0 70–30, and it went 201–98 on the public Gen 1 OU ladder against humans (peak rating 1595) before the account was battle-banned on 29 Oct.

Generalization: three sets of teams we never trained on (A, B and a new C), plus our own teams new to Metamon, and it wins on all of them. The giant run (+4B steps on 3,605 teams from every Metamon team set, 30% of games drafting freely) turned free-pick from a 41% loss to Kakuna into a 74% win, while piloting held or improved. Metamon trained on about 5.3M human replays plus self-play over the same team sets.

Final model (29 Oct): the ladder run (this 4B checkpoint + 2B steps on 4,150 teams, including every team ladder players used against us). It beats the 4B checkpoint head to head, 52.1% and 51.8% on two teams (~5,000 games each, ±1.4). The Metamon battery above is the 4B checkpoint's; it was not re-run on the final model. Final ladder team: the round-robin winner (Team comps tab), 29–12 on the ladder, peak 1594.

What the full game means

  • A Pokémon battle has two halves: team building and piloting. Team building picks six Pokémon, four moves each, and a lead. Piloting is playing the battle turn by turn.
  • Mirror tests remove team building. Both sides get the same teams with the same moves, so only piloting is compared.
  • The full game keeps both halves. Each side brings the teams it would choose. Metamon brings its "competitive" team set: 28 complete human-made teams (species and exact movesets), the set its authors use for their own evaluations and ladder play. We bring ours, in one of two ways:
    • OMNI with a Smogon sample team (the full-game column, 69.0–88.5%): zam_egg_cloy_don — Alakazam, Exeggutor, Cloyster, Rhydon, Chansey, Tauros, lead Alakazam — taken from the Smogon RBY OU sample-teams thread. Our policy picks every move itself (no Smogon movesets), then pilots.
    • Pure exploration (free-pick): the policy builds the whole team from scratch — any of the 151 Gen 1 Pokémon, any legal moves — and pilots it. Nothing comes from Smogon or Metamon.

What each test means

  • Mirror test (piloting skill). Both sides use the same set of teams with identical movesets. For every pair of teams (X, Y) we pilot X while Metamon pilots Y, then the sides swap with the same battle seed. Team strength and luck cancel, so the win rate measures who plays the same teams better.
  • Unseen teams. Teams sampled from Metamon's own ladder and replay team sets, checked to be absent from every one of our training pools. We never trained on them. Metamon learned from human replays, so these replay-derived teams are, if anything, closer to its training data than to ours. One comparison, not a double count: the same unseen teams are used by both sides, and we pilot them better.
  • Full game (team building + piloting). Each side brings its own teams: Metamon plays its 28 hand-built competitive teams (its own choice), we play ours. The free-pick row drafted its team from any legal Gen 1 species and moves, not from Metamon's teams.
  • Before this run: OMNI-2 trial 11 (trained on 19 teams) was below 50% in the mirror test on Metamon's competitive teams (45.8 / 51.5) and only 53.3 / 60.2 on unseen teams A. Training on more teams with given sets is what made the piloting general.

Caveats and next checks

  • Kakuna (Metamon's current best) is beaten on every test by the 4B checkpoint, including the new held-out set C (54.8%) and free-pick (74.3%). KakunaEnsemble and TaurosEnsemble (ensembles that reached #1 on the Showdown ladder) are untested.
  • Checked on real Showdown. Most tests run in our native Gen 1 engine; the Showdown bridge confirmed the full-game results on a real Showdown server (Kakuna 68–32 vs 69.0 native, TaurosV0 70–30 vs 73.0), and the bot plays the public ladder.
  • Specialist vs generalist. Metamon spreads its capacity over several generations; ours is Gen 1 OU only.
  • Sampling vs argmax. Metamon plays its default sampled policy, ours argmax. For ours it makes no difference: the final model against itself, argmax vs sampling, 51.0% / 49.8% with seats swapped (~9,200 games each).
  • Size (done): the giant recipe from scratch for 2B steps, held-out A vs Kakuna: 512×2 49.2, 512×3 46.2, 2048×2 52.5 (±2.5). Width helps a little; depth 3 does not. Our 9.6M 1024×2 reached 57.5 only through a much longer lineage.
  • Final model not re-run on this battery. The ladder-run model beats the 4B checkpoint 52% head to head, so it is likely at least as strong here; untested.

What we know so far

  • Playing many teams makes a better pilot. OMNI_MASTER, one policy across all 13 league teams, beats each team's own specialist on every team scored (best 71.2 / 67.4).
  • Free-pick works when it starts from OMNI and mostly plays itself. It explored thousands of teams, settled on a standard OU team (Alakazam / Chansey / Lapras / Rhydon / Starmie / Tauros) with near-textbook sets, and scored 62.8 / 56.6. Every earlier free-pick run peaked near 40.
  • Earlier free-pick was capped by its team, not its moves. Its Jolteon / Articuno team trained B1-style with species fixed scored 34.9. Move pairs fixed the sets (4% → 28% exact Smogon) without raising the score.
  • League games matter, and EMAgnet helps. 25% frozen-league games beat pure self-play by 9 to 24 points; magnet off cost about 10.
  • Bigger nets need more steps. 1024×2 started far behind 256×2 and caught up by 750M; after a warm restart it reached 65.3 / 56.6.
  • A stronger league alone did not help (league gen 2), and window or entity-Transformer memory did not beat MinGRU with the league.
  • Seed noise is about ±3–5 points on one run, so gaps under 5 need a second seed.
  • Kakuna is a bad judge of teams (29 Oct). Teams that beat Kakuna most (four sleepers) lose to strong teams in our own round robin; the round-robin winner was our best ladder team (71%).
  • Full free-pick collapses onto one team. puffermon_v4 (+2B steps, every seat drafting) drafted the old peak team 12 of 12 times and got worse on other teams (43.9% on the round-robin winner).
  • More of the same training gives little: +2B steps on 4,150 teams gave 52% over the 4B model. Width 2048 gave +3 over 512 at equal steps; depth 3 lost.
  • Argmax and sampling are equal for our policy (it is nearly deterministic).
  • Humans adapt: the regular Nivky (56 games) moved through four team styles against us; by-team ladder results swing within noise.

Research direction: OMNI training and the Kirby loop

Key finding: a generalist beats specialists at their own teams. Did one specialist learn its team better than a policy trained on every strategy at once? No. OMNI, trained on all 13 league teams (a team drawn per game), beats each team's own specialist on that specialist's team (OMNI-1024: 77.1 / 69.4 on zam_egg_cloy_don vs its specialist's 58.3 / 58.0). And specialists do not transfer: B0 gar scores 62.5 / 57.6 on its own team and 2–51 on the other twelve. Being good at everything made it better at each thing.

OMNI training: train one policy on all strategies of a strategy space through reset states that commit it to one strategy per episode. EMAgnet keeps several strategies alive in a non-transitive game instead of collapsing to one.

The Kirby loop (working name), closest to Naruto's shadow clones: send clones out to explore (free-pick drafts from OMNI), harvest what they learn (a discovered team + sets becomes a new reset state), and the main policy grows as useful states accumulate. Accept a strategy only if OMNI keeps its scores on the others, keep a small forced league of diverse exploiters, repeat, and step up in model size over time. Long-term question: can one OMNI policy be grown this way from rules bots and forced states, then applied to a harder environment?

Loop design (to sweep, not fixed)

  • Stints: continuous training in ~250M-step stints from the last checkpoint; between stints a script harvests teams, updates the pool, league and draft ban list, and runs evals.
  • Game mix: pool teams + a free-draft share (explore) + league opponents. Every share and threshold here is a starting guess to sweep.
  • Harvest: a team + sets that holds a large share of free drafts and wins ≥50% vs the league joins the pool and the league; that exact team is then banned from free drafts so exploration keeps finding new ones.
  • Annealing: constant LR + EMAgnet while exploring; anneal to finalize a release policy.
  • Weighting toward weak teams: only for exploiters; for the main policy it can backfire in a non-transitive game, so sweep it.
  • League: kept small. One main policy pilots every team; exploiters only need to find its weaknesses.
  • Metamon is a held-out monitor. Teams are selected by league and self-play results, never by Metamon score.

Future directions

  • Counter-team finder: once the pool is large, rank teams by subsection and answer "given this opponent team (± moves), what is the best team to counter it?"
  • Real Pokémon PvP and gym challenges: the same OMNI + exploration loop on in-game battles, e.g. "if every trainer had level-X Pokémon, what is the best team for the gym leaders?", with videos of the results.
  • Harder environments after Metamon is clearly beaten.

Shared Transformer speed-up

The shared Transformer spent 41% of its time in softmax and LayerNorm kernels that left most GPU threads idle at our small sizes. Switching them to one warp per row made it 1.5× faster at the Pokémon size and 1.8× at the Goofspiel size. The math is unchanged, and the FP64-reference, BF16, CUDA-graph and Pokémon parity tests all pass. The change is logged in the core changes audit.
